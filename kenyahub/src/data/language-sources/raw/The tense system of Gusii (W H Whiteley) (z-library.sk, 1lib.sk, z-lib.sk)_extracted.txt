=== INTRODUCTION ===
The Gusii,[1] numbering 255,000 in 1948, occupy a fertile area of high country[2] in the South Nyanza District of Kenya. The people are both pastoral and agricultural, providing a substantial part of Kenya's maize and an increasing quantity of coffee. Between them and Lake Victoria are their traditional enemies the Nilotic Luo. To the east and south-east they are bordered by the Nilo-Hamitic Kipsigis and Maasai, also numbered among their enemies by tradition. To the south, but separated by a corridor of Luo, are the closely related Kuria, known in Kenya as Tende.
Anthropologically the Gusii are known through the writings of Dr. P. Mayer,[3] but linguistically they are little known, and the present writer's Introduction to the language (1956) was the first published work in this hitherto rather neglected language. In his Classification of the Bantu Languages (1948) Professor Guthrie groups Gusii (E42),[4] along with Kuria (E43) and Logooli (E41), in Group 40 of Zone E, to which most of the other Bantu languages of Kenya and Uganda also belong.
It is generally believed, both among the people themselves and outsiders, that among the peoples of Kenya, it is with the Logooli, Kuria (Tende) and Gīkūyū (E51) that they have the closest affinity. Linguistically there are a number of features which seem to confirm such a view (e.g. general phonemic structure, especially the 'seven-vowel' system). There are also, however, some other features—which are not shared by Gīkūyū or Kamba—which seem to suggest that Gusii, along with Kuria and Logooli, may have affinities with the Luyia (E30) and Uganda languages of E10 (e.g. VCV-shape of nominal prefixes, position of the negative sign ti-).
The evidence of oral tradition lends credence to such a view; all accounts agree that the Gusii moved into their present habitat from the north between 14 and 18 generations ago, leaving the related Logooli in North Nyanza on the way, and subsequently losing the Tende, particularly the abaSweta, who moved on further south. Such a view is by no means incompatible with the linguistic evidence of a link with Gīkūyū if one supposes that the Gusii moved south only after reaching the Mt. Elgon area from central Kenya. They would thus have moved in a wide sweep to the east and north of the Nandi/Kipsigis, who in the oral traditions seem to have been in their present habitat when the Gusii first moved south from the area of the Uganda border.
Where, then, do the Kuria fit in with such a supposition? Dr. M. J. Ruel,[5] who has recently completed an anthropological study, reports traditions of origin which suggest a double stream; a northerly one from the area of South Nyanza, and an easterly one from the Serengeti, from the area of the Ngoreme and Ikoma, with whom the Kuria have clear linguistic affinities. The northerly stream accords well with Gusii traditions, but there is nothing which will fit in with the easterly one. Linguistically, Kuria is characterized by some features differentiating it both from Gusii and Gīkūyū (i.e. the post-radical suffix -ete, -ite does not occur); on the other hand the range of tense signs is rather nearer to Gīkūyū than to Gusii. The evidence must be regarded as inconclusive at the moment.

Consideration of such points in traditional history is not irrelevant to the study even of such a patently specialized topic as verbal systems. There is no reason to suppose that the groups as they are at present are homogeneous in origin, indeed there is much to suggest that in earlier times groups came together and subsequently dispersed—or remained together—in widely differing circumstances and patterns. Under such circumstances one might expect to find linguistic evidence for such events. Yet it is only by making a very detailed analysis that the salient features of a particular system emerge and provide a possible line of investigation for corroborating or revising evidence adduced from other fields of enquiry. Some of the implications of the present study are discussed in Section 9.0.
The material is here divided conventionally into phonology, morphology and syntax, and represents an attempt to furnish a formal description of the Gusii tense-system, in the belief that this represents the most economical way of describing the system exhaustively. Some discussion of terms employed and their abbreviation may be relevant at this point.
Firstly, some reference should be made to the terms 'radical' and 'stem'. Radical (R) here denotes the irreducible element of the verbal (e.g. -rúg-/Cook): it has elsewhere been referred to as the 'root' or 'core'. 'Stem' denotes the non-prefix element in a nominal (e.g. -te/Tree). Nominals are of two kinds, designated respectively 'long-series' and 'short-series'. Short-series nominals (SsN), elsewhere referred to as 'nouns', are those nominal stems which are commonly associated with a relatively limited series of prefixes. These are commonly identified in terms of the singular/plural dichotomy, thus 'ómo-te, éme-te/Tree, trees'. The term 'relatively' is important, however, since many stems may be associated with several such pairs of prefixes, thus:—
ómo-te, éme-te/Tree(s)
ríímote, ámamote/Large tree(s)
égete, ébite/Twig(s)
ágate, ébite/Small twig(s)
óbote (rarely !)/Small twigs
Long-series nominals (LsN), by definition, may be associated with a relatively longer series of prefixes, a full series in many cases. Into this category fall adjectives, demonstratives and possessives, which can be distinguished as sub-categories according to the shape of their prefixes. Finally, one must take account of invariable words, which are never associated with simple verbal or nominal prefixes, and which are outside the system of agreement or control.
In the phonological discussion the letters V and C denote any vowel or consonant, but in later sections V denotes any verbal. This may be refined by adding numerals, thus V1, V2, etc., to indicate whether the verbal in question occurs first or second in the utterance under consideration.

=== 1.0. PHONOLOGY ===

1.1. There are in Gusii 14 vowel phonemes: seven short and seven long. They may be exemplified as follows:—
        Front    Back
High    i, ii    u, uu
High/Mid e, ee   o, oo
Low/Mid  ɛ, ɛɛ   ɔ, ɔɔ
Low      a, aa
All vowel phonemes occur in all positions but 'i, ii' and 'u, uu' occur infrequently initially. All vowel phonemes may bear a high, low and mid-level tone, or a falling or rising tone.

Long vowels do not occur in the nominal prefix system but do so commonly in that of verbals. They also occur contrastively in verbal radicals, thus:—
-sik- Respect           -siik- Prepare beer
-bas- Elope             -baas- Start skinning a beast
-ber- Be boiling        -béér- Sit, sit down [1]
-tɛ́m- Hit               -tɛɛm- Try
-kɔn- Bewitch           -kɔɔn- Be annoyed at something
-bór- Lack              -bóór- Say
-kur- Butt              -kuur- Cry

1.11. Vowel sequence: Internal, immediate
In general, all sequences retain their individual tones within a given radical or stem. Where the first member of a sequence (i, o, u) is a component of an affix, it tends to be realized as 'y' or 'w'.[2] The tones of members of dissimilar vowel sequences are realized as falling, if the tone of the first component is high (-tó--à- as -twâ-), and as rising or high if the tone of the second component is high (-tó--á- as -twá OR akà-ébâ as akéébâ). Sequences of the negative pre-prefix 'ti-' and a following verbal-prefix 'o' or 'a' are realized as 'to, ta'. Sequences of similar vowels, where either component is long, are characterized by a very slight pause, noted in the following pages by '.' (see below):
i-  -tiók-/Give off a smell, ókonia/Excreting, náániete/He excreted, ríí.íico/Flock, ríí.íga/Cooking stone.[3]
e-  -rugeire/Have cooked for, éke.engwe/Stump of a tree, éámáte/Kinship group, ékeoore/Skull, éubi/Dirt.
ɛ-  -rɛɛtɛire/Have brought for, ékɛ.ɛɛra/A half, éɔɔra/Ravine.
a-  óborwáire/Sickness, náɛra/I finished, -táóri-/Ladle out, náuuta/I blew on the fire, áráá.áákwe/He will be beaten (cf. áráákwe/He will die).
NOTE: The sequence a—e (short vowel only) is realized as 'ee'; similarly, the sequence a—o is realized as 'oo':—nééba/I forgot, okéébá/You forgot, yóóma/It was dry, akóóma/He was dry, ámééŋe (Cl.6)/Short ones.

Sequences of more than two vowels commonly occur:—
ébiááo (Cl.8)/Others, ciáéta (Cl.10)/They passed, ókoéórókereria/Teaching oneself.

(b) ɔCɔ, ɛCɛ, ɛ concomitantly with stem shape (V)CV . . . where V is ɛ OR ɔ

1.22. Clusters: Two sets of clusters commonly occur: nasal clusters and 'w' clusters.

1.221. The following nasal clusters occur: mb, nc, nd, nt, nk, ng, ns, nj.

The 'b, g' or 'mb, ng' are realized as stops not as fricatives.

1.3. Tone
If one accepts the 'phoneme' as a useful concept, one might consider the usefulness of an analogous term like 'toneme', being the minimal unit of pitch which serves to distinguish words; thus in Gusii one finds:—
-rók-/Vomit         -rok-/Call
-róót-/Dream        -rɔɔt-/Investigate
-bár-/Shine         -bar-/Count
Such examples occur far less frequently than those in which a distinction of tone serves to distinguish formally similar tenses. It is accepted that tenses so distinguished are as much different words as those illustrated above, but there is the additional feature that they are grammatically as well as lexically distinct.
Two level tonemes are recognized: a high (´) and a low (unmarked). There may be a case for recognizing a third, mid, toneme (¯), though in the majority of contexts this may be regarded as either a raised low or a lowered high. A falling tone, from high to low (ˆ), is indicated over a long vowel by an acute accent over the first mora. A rising tone, restricted to long vowels, is indicated thus (ˇ), or by acute accent over the second mora.

1.4. Prominence
Prominence tends to occur on the radical if this is of a shape (VC, CVC, CVVC, CV), and on the first syllable of long radicals. In such longer radicals there may be a subsidiary prominence on the final syllable.

=== 2.0. MORPHOLOGY OF THE VERBAL ===

Verbals, as distinct from other categories, are characterized by irreducible radicals, and by a considerable range of pre- and post-radical elements which are commutable within the word.

2.1. The simple radical
The shape of the simple radical is commonly -V(V)C-, -CV(V)-, -CV(V)C-, -CV(V)CV(V)C-, but other shapes from -Z- to -CV(V)CV(V)- and CV(V)CV(V)CV(V)- also occur.
It is observable at an early stage of investigation that comparable verbal shapes are associated with different tone-patterns, which can be satisfactorily explained only on the assumption that different radicals are associated with different 'basic' tones; indeed, as has been noted already, some radicals are distinguished only by tone. Radicals may thus be divided up into Groups according to their 'basic' tones, as deduced from their distinctive tonal behaviour. Distinctive patterns are not, however, characteristic of every tense. Many tenses, about 30 per cent. of one-word tenses, are associated with a pattern common to all Groups, which must probably be ascribed to the tense, rather than to any particular component of the verbal. These patterns will hereafter be referred to as Imposed Patterns (IP). They occur particularly in B tenses and in those others where tonal patterns are the only distinctive feature (vide C2/1, C2/3). With the exception of such patterns the so-called basic tones appear consistently on the radical. The Groups may be set out as follows:—

Group I 'High':
Ia  -CVC-, -CV-, -VC- (-rók-/Vomit, -ít-/Kill)
Ib  -CVVC-, -CVV-, -VVC- (-réét-/Bring, -áák-/Hit)
Ic  -CVCVC-, -VCVC-, -CVCV- (-tákún-/Chew)

Group II 'Low':
IIa -CVC-, -CV-, -VC- (-rok-/Call, -ni-/Excrete)
IIb -CVVC-, -CVV-, -VVC- (-caak-/Begin, -maa-/See)
IIc -CVCVC-, -VCVC-, CVCV(V)- (-tugut-/Throw down, -amuum-/Make a noise like a hyena)

Group III 'High/Low':
-VCV(V)C- (-gwénɛr-/Be pleasing)

Group IV:
-C-, -Z- (-Z-/Give, -c-/Come, -a-/Chop up vegetables)

The position in Gusii, thus differs considerably from related languages. In Gīkūyū, the 'basic' tone of the radical is described as being realized on the syllable immediately following the radical, through a process known as tone-shift.[1] By contrast, in the closely related Kuria, tone-classes in verbals do not properly occur at all, since the tone of any radical may be inferred from its shape: radicals of a shape -CVVC- all being high-toned. The position is also different from that in some languages recently described by Belgian writers, who have found it necessary to describe speech-tones in terms of stated realizations of hypothesized structural tones.[2] The method adopted here for Gusii is clearly not the only method, but does seem to be the simplest which accounts satisfactorily for the facts as recorded, though it does leave the Imposed Pattern as a residual category.

-na-  Not occurring as a simple sign and occurring only in A tenses. Not associated with time reference but with 'continuity'.

=== 3.0. CLASSIFICATION OF ONE-WORD TENSES ===

Two main criteria may be adopted as a starting point for classifying tenses: shape and meaning. In the past, meaning has seemed to be the most convenient basis for such a task, but it has been less the meaning of a particular tense which has served as the basis for the label, so much as the proximity of the meaning to that covered by a Latin or Classical label.[1] This is particularly true where verb-forms are further categorized into 'moods' and 'voices'.[2] There is something to be said for using descriptive labels for tenses when teaching a language, provided that too much store is not set by thinking that the label has a one-to-one correlation with a specific meaning. In some languages, however, there may be almost as many forms as basic meanings, so that tenses treated according to meaning may provide a useful and relatively simple method of classification.[3]

Footnotes to Section 3.0:
1. See my 'Swahili and the Classical Tradition', T.N.R. No. 52, 1959.
2. Even amongst those who accept the need for a different approach to the description of grammatical categories, there may yet be a reluctance to depart from these well-known labels. Cf. 'The Grammatical Structure of Zulu', A. T. Cope, African St., Vol. XVI, 1957, pp. 218-20.
3. See, for example, 'The Tabulation of Tenses in a Bantu Language', J. C. Sharman, Africa, Vol. XXVI, 1956, pp. 29-46.

If, on the other hand, one adopts a classification by shape, this does at least have the merit of accounting for all the relevant tenses without the fear of being misled by labels. Difficulties do arise, however, as soon as one wishes to make use of a schema worked out in one language, for a second, and perhaps closely related, language. While accurate and detailed descriptions of individual languages are both necessary and desirable, it would seem to be equally desirable for the descriptions to be effected in a manner which may make it possible to abstract comparable features from other languages in order to reach a new level of classificatory description. For example, in comparison with the well-established system of classifying nominal classes, classifications of tenses appear arbitrary and sterile. This is due, in large measure, to the nature of the data; while the number of distinct pre- and post-radical series of elements is very limited, the shapes of tense signs, for example, vary enormously, and it becomes difficult to decide what criteria should be adopted for establishing differences. The point may usefully be considered here.
Difficulties of classification by shape are well exemplified by examining Kuria, a language closely related to Gusii, and Gusii itself. There are in Kuria three tense signs not occurring at all in Gusii, in which in turn there is one tense sign and a whole range of tenses with final -ete which do not occur in the other language. Again, in Kuria, verbal tone classes cannot be established, while they are readily established in Gusii. Which of these factors must be deemed particularly significant in assessing differences between the two verbal systems? What kind of 'weighting' must be adopted? Is it more important that a tense vp-a-Radical-ire occurs in both languages than that its connotation is different in each case?
In attempting to answer such questions, it must first be stated that a general classification by shape seems preferable to one based on meaning; one is, after all, primarily concerned with differences between different structures rather than between different interpretations of those structures. It would also seem possible that a statement of general features; for example, numbers of tense signs and their capacity for combination, may be more profitable than discussion of specific features such as the shape of tense signs. In addition to this the number of post-radical suffixes may be significant, together with distinctions of tense by tone, and the degree to which the differentiation of past and future time is, or is not, symmetrical.
To this end a first classification of the Gusii material is made by considering the four post-radical elements:—
-a      -e      -ire    -ete
Tenses characterised by such suffixes are labelled A, B, C and D respectively. Numbers are then allocated to specific signs, thus:—
1 Zero sign
2 -a-
4 -ka-
7 -na- (multiple signs only)
9 -raa-
(NOTE: The intervening numbers occur in Kuria only, and the spaces are retained as the schema is being used for both languages.)
Where two or more tenses are differentiated only by tone, the symbols /1 and /2 are adopted, thus:—
2/1 -á-         2/2 -a-
It would be perfectly possible to treat /2 as an additional tense sign, but the present device is adopted here for convenience.
Where a tense is characterized by an initial 'n-', the number is followed by an 'a', i.e. A2a. It is worth noting that such a feature occurs only in positive tenses. (Exc. APx1a.)

It has not yet proved possible to assign—without exceptions—a specific tone to each of the tense signs, since, as will be noted from the synopses, there is some variation. As noted above, it may well be that with -ka-, for example, one should posit a plurality of signs, each with a specific tone—and meaning.
Each sign may occur in combination with other signs and this reaches an extreme form in tense Px25975 vp-ta-a-ko-raa-na-ko-Radical-a.
Negative tenses are labelled initially P, Q, R or S, by analogy with the positive tenses. Those which prove to be comparable to a positive tense both in shape and meaning are designated both by the negative and the positive letter, together with the appropriate number. Tenses comparable either in shape OR meaning are designated by the negative letter only. Tenses comparable neither in shape nor in meaning are designated by a negative letter in lower case. Tenses with the negative sign -tá- are, in addition, labelled with an 'x' following the negative letter.
No division of the material is made into 'moods', nor is there any time/aspect dichotomy. All verb-forms in which a full series of verbal prefixes occur are labelled tenses, and the term should be understood on that basis. It will be abundantly clear from the examples that many features other than time are represented in the tenses, but no satisfactory classification seems practicable on any other basis.

Notes on One-Word Tenses:
It is clear that one of the more striking features in this system is the number of tenses distinguished only by tone: 21 pairs and two triplets. In the majority of these pairs the distinction is realized at a semantic level by the division into 'near' and 'less near' time.
An additional interesting feature, not indicated in the synopsis, is the association of D2a/1 with negative connotations where characterized by a negative tone-pattern but not by negative tense signs.
The following features seem worthy of note among pre- and post-radical elements:—
-raa- Always low-toned, except in Imposed Patterns (five examples) and D29a/2, where the pattern -ráa- occurs. Occurs in 21 tenses. With the exception of A29/2—which is in any case of doubtful occurrence—the element is always in tonal contrast with the preceding element.
-na-  Occurs in 15 tenses—all A tenses—and in every case is followed by -ko-. Only in two of these is -na- low-toned, and in both of these cases -ko- also is low-toned. On the other hand in six other tenses -kó- occurs with a high tone, and it does not yet seem possible to account satisfactorily for the occurrence of low tones.
-ka-  Occurring in three positive A tenses only, and in each case is low-toned. It does not occur in B tenses, but in C tenses the position is more difficult. Here the sign occurs only with -á- and -a- pairs (three in all). In the positive pair the tone of -á- appears to double on to the -ka-, but in the two negative pairs the doubling occurs only in CRx24/1 and 2: in the other pair the tone of -ka- is in contrast to -a-. In D tenses, where the sign is not preceded by -a-, the tone is high. In the one pair where it is associated with -a-, then, as noted above, the tone appears to double on to the -ka-.
In considering the suffixes, the -a, with one single exception, is low. B tenses form an Imposed Pattern series. In C tenses the positive pattern of -ire is high/low, while in the negative it is low/low. This is also partly true of D tenses, but here the position is complicated for low-toned radicals (see 2.44) and Sx2/1 appears in any case to be anomalous.
There appears to be no consistency in the tenses characterized by Imposed Patterns, except in B tenses, where all are so characterized. Of the four tenses in A, only two belong to tonally distinct pairs. Of the seven examples in C tenses, six belong to tonally distinct pairs but not consistently either to a 'near' or 'less near' member. Of the three examples in D tenses, two belong to tonally distinct pairs.

=== 4.0. TWO-WORD TENSES ===

The term two-word tenses is used to describe those tenses characterized by two distinct radicals. Only one of these is associated with a full series of verbal prefixes; the other is invariable. The connotation of the whole is associated with that of the invariable component, which commonly, but by no means invariably, follows the other. Usually no piece may intervene between the two components. Of the radicals associated with a full verbal-prefix series, the following occur most frequently:—
-re, -is-, -many-, -b-, -c-, -ci-
Of these the occurrence of the second and third is restricted to such contexts.
The same scheme of identification is adopted as for one-word tenses with the addition of:—

=== 5.0. THE SYNTAX OF GUSII TENSES ===
One- and two-word tenses in context.
Having established the range of tenses which may occur, it remains to consider the contexts in which they occur. Primarily, one is concerned with linguistic contexts, though it must be noted that certain linguistic contexts themselves occur only in specific social contexts (vide Px25, A9), which might well form the basis for a further examination.
For the purpose of the present study the context selected is that of the sentence,[1] taken in many instances from conversation occurring in daily life around the home, school and courthouse. In some cases examples have been taken from texts.[2]

5.1. Sentences seem broadly divisible, from the point of view of the present study, into three main categories, of which the last may be subdivided:—
1. Non-verbal sentences.
2. Uni-verbal sentences.
3. Multi-verbal sentences.
   (a) In which control operates between a single short-series nominal and a succession of verbal prefixes.
   (b) In which control operates between several such nominals and successive verbal prefixes.
We shall not be concerned here with non-verbal sentences, and uni-verbal sentences present few problems. It must be noted, however, that more than half the total of one-word tenses may occur in such sentences, of which the minimal pattern is exemplified by a single verbal:—
C2/1 ogúúre/He has fallen
B1a  nágēndɛ/He will go
At the syntactic level, therefore, one may re-group these one-word tenses into a new Class, of which they form the only members.

5.2. All other tenses can occur only in multi-verbal sentences, which represent by far the commoner type of sentence in folk-tales and narrative generally, as well as occurring widely in conversation. Tenses may be classified both according to their range of occurrence and according to the tenses with which they may be associated.

5.21. Consider, first, the following examples, in which control operates between a single short-series nominal and a succession of verbal prefixes:—
1. ómoísék(e) ɔréétire cínko korénde táraabuuca‿ámááce (C2/1, P9)
   The girl has brought the firewood but has not drawn the water.
2. ómoísék(e) ɔréétire cínko árúgere‿éndáágera (C2/1, B1)
   The girl has brought firewood to cook food with.
3. ómoíséke narééta cínko,[3] onyv́órirwe (A1a, C2/1)
   The girl although she is bringing firewood she is late.
4. ómoíséke náraarééta cínko áámanya korúg(a) éndáágera (A29/1)
   The girl fetched firewood and then cooked food.
5. ékɛrɔ ómoísék(e) ɔréétire cínko, akarúg(a) éndáágera (C2/1, A4)
   When the girl had brought firewood she cooked food.

Of these 1 may be divided into two segments, each a complete utterance, linked by a connector, in this case 'korénde'. It is noteworthy that a translation of the segments plus the connector will serve as a translation of the whole. Other connectors include 'Naénde/Also, then'; 'Ne/Also, and'; 'ério/Then, whereupon'. This last commonly occurs initially in the sentence, but occurs as a connector especially following certain radicals, notably those connoting 'saying, telling, ordering, etc.' (see Examples 12 and 13 below). Example 2 may also be divided into two segments, each a complete utterance; but here there is no connector and the translation of the segments will not serve as a translation for the whole. Example 3 cannot be divided at all; tense A1a can never occur in a uni-verbal sentence, but must be associated with a second, commonly following, verbal.[1] Example 4 likewise cannot be divided, and here neither of the tenses may occur in a uni-verbal sentence. The same may be said of Example 5, though here A4 may occur in a uni-verbal sentence provided that there is a close link between it and a preceding sentence.

The general relationship which obtains between verbals in Examples 3-5, I am terming concomitance, and tenses exemplifying such a relationship are termed concomitant tenses. Concomitance is commonly of the asymmetrical variety exemplified here, where the presence of A entails B but not vice versa, since C2/1 (Example 3) may also occur in uni-verbal sentences. Furthermore, concomitance is rarely of an absolute variety, that is to say only between tense X and tense Y; commonly it is rather between tense X and tenses Y, Z and A, and the range of concomitant tenses for these three will, of course, differ again. Symmetrical concomitance, in which the presence of A entails B and vice versa, is also rather rare, but may be exemplified by the following:—
6. onyé totáasimegetí ámaɛmba, angá éncará njâtoria; where the first two words, and the fourth and last words, are in a relation-ship of symmetrical concomitance.

While Examples 1 and 2 do not exemplify concomitance in the sense defined above, they do exemplify a co-occurrence: for example, where C2/1 is followed by 'korénde', then only a narrow range of tenses can occur as V2, i.e. P9, P5/1, P1/Ai1. In contrast, if C2/1 were followed by 'naénde' then a different and wider range of tenses may occur. Finally, and most important, such tenses may also occur in uni-verbal sentences. Where they occur in multi-verbal sentences they may be said to be 'in attendance' on such other tenses as co-occur in the sentence.

Thus, two kinds of tense have now been discussed: firstly, those which occur only in multi-verbal sentences and which occur concomitantly with other tenses. As will be seen from the detailed examination of the tenses this concomitance may be restricted to one or two tenses or be fairly extensive. Secondly, there are tenses which occur in attendance with other tenses but which may occur in uni-verbal sentences.

One pattern of multi-verbal sentence of this kind has not yet been mentioned; consider the following example:—
7. ómoísék(e) ɔkɔrééta cínko onyóórirwe
   The girl (who is) bringing firewood is late.

Is this to be considered a multi-verbal or a uni-verbal sentence? It is clear that in referring to uni-verbal or multi-verbal sentences at all, one is speaking in morphological terms, in the sense that—as in the above examples—the pieces may be identified by their characteristic shapes. The boundaries of categories as delimited by formal criteria may not, however, be co-extensive with those as delimited by other criteria: there is often some overlapping.[2] For example, the distribution of a given verb tense may be comparable to that of a long-series nominal, and at this level may need to be grouped with it. The foregoing remarks may now be re-stated in the following terms: that the exponents of morphological categories, which must, of necessity, occur in linear sequences,[3] may be re-classified according to distribution, which involves a study both of position and relationships. At this point it is primarily position which must be discussed, and in particular the extent to which members of the same morphological category may occupy similar or dissimilar positions to those occupied by members of other categories. For example, the position between a short-series nominal and a following verbal, whose verbal prefix is controlled by that nominal, may be occupied by a long-series nominal; adjective, demonstrative or possessive, thus:—
8. ómwán(a) orwárire/The child is sick
   ómwán(a) ori(a) orwárire/That child is sick
   ómwán(a) ori(a) ómoké orwárire/That small child is sick

In addition, however, verbals in certain tenses may also occur:—
   ómwán(a) okórer(a) orwárire/The child who is crying is sick
   ómoísék(e) ɔkɔrééta cínko onyóórirwe/Vide sup.

Items which occupy such a position in relation to a preceding short-series nominal clearly have some claim to be regarded as members of a common Distribution-Class. Provisionally, a Substitution-Class I is set up,[4] for membership in which, the following tenses qualify: A2/1, A2/2, Px2/1, Px2/2, A5, Px9, B9, Qx2/3, C1/1, CRx2/1, C2/2, C2/3, D2/1, D2/2, Sx2/1, Sx2/2. It must be realized, however, that the range of occurrence of these tenses is not restricted to membership of such a Substitution-Class. This will be seen clearly from the detailed examination of tenses, but the following examples from A5 may serve as illustration:—
9. ékɛr(ɔ) ábanto bágósóa nyomba, ábáána bákóbasɛka/When the people enter the room, the children laugh at them.
   akamobóória ŋai ógócia?/He asked him—where are you going?

In none of the cases are the tenses members of such a Substitution-Class, the only pieces for which 'bágósóa, bákóbasɛka, ógócia' may be substituted are verbals.

Nevertheless, the importance of such a phenomenon in sentence structure lies in its being a factor of sentence extension. A verbal which is a member of such a Class may be followed by a series of subsequent short-series nominals, each with a potentiality for further extension. While such extension is theoretically limited only by the breath capacity of the speaker, in practice sentences seem commonly restricted to two or three complexes[5] in such a post short-series nominal position. The following examples illustrate some of the more extreme examples recorded:—
10. ori(a) ómorioori ig(ɔ) árenge korééta ámááce(e) áábéékw(a) égetega, ááyia, áábera
    The diviner used to bring water which was placed in a gourd, put on the fire, heated, and brought to the boil.
11. akáéba ámaŋáná ari(a) écíngi ámokáánetie ŋa táácia kobatɛɛbia ŋa nárieerete cíngi — akabatɛɛbia....
    He forgot the matter of the flies, which he (his friend) had forbidden him to go and tell his relatives about his having eaten them, and he told them....

What is true for the number of complexes which commonly occur, is also true for the number of non-substitution tenses in such sentences, though long sequences of A2/1, A4 or B1 are not uncommon in narrative:—
12. ábagááka bánde bakabéâ bakabóóra bákórɛ‿ésɛɛgi ério báŋāānye bakarwa arórɔ (A4, B1)
    Some elders became very angry and said they should declare war and move their houses and leave there.
13. ábanto tibáárenge koba nómɔrɛmbɛ báíkaranse bárēme — ério bányɔ̄ɔ̄rɛ rííbaga bákóremer(a) éndáágera bárie báárwo báténenke
    The people had no peace to settle down and cultivate, whereby to get an opportunity for cultivating their food to eat, so that they might leave the area and become wealthy.

5.22. Multi-verbal sentences in which control operates between several short-series nominals and successive verbal prefixes, occur commonly and are of several different kinds. The commonest patterns are those in which a complex is preceded by some word such as 'ékɛrɔ/When'; 'buna/How'; 'ékiágɛra/Because'; 'korénde/But'; 'ŋa/That'. Of these 'korénde, ékiágɛra' have been referred to earlier as connectors, linking two otherwise complete utterances. There is no limit, other than the speaker's breath capacity, to the number of sequences that can be linked, but it is rare to find the number exceeding three. Of the remaining three words, all are important factors of sentence extension, and in all cases the sequence or complex following, may have substituted for them a single word; a short-series nominal in the case of 'buna, ŋa' and an invariable word of time in the case of 'ékɛrɔ'. Two new Substitution-Classes may therefore need to be set up; one for short-series nominals in any position, and the other for invariable time-words, which might later prove capable of extension to other invariable words. Consider the following examples:—
1. ómoísék(e) ɔréétire cínko korénde momur(a) ómwabo táraabuuca‿ámááce
   The girl has brought the firewood but her brother has not drawn the water.
2. tímanyeti buna tórááce kɔgɛnda mambia, ékiágɛr(a) ényange yane yabúnekire
   I do not know how we shall go tomorrow as my bicycle is broken.
3. ígɔ twásɛɛmɛretie ŋa ndɛɛró árááce
   We trust he will come today.
4. ékɛr(ɔ) ágwet(e) ábáána bagasɛka
   When he fell down the children laughed.

The following substitutions may occur:—
2. tímanyeti ámaŋána‿ámange ékiágɛra tíndaamanya gosoomа
   I don't know much because I haven't learned to read.
3. ígɔ twásɛɛmɛretie ámagɛsa‿ámaya
   We trust there'll be a good harvest.
4. éri(o) ábáána bagasɛka
   Whereupon the children laughed.

In addition, however, a number of tenses occur in contexts which seem to make possible a substitution, for example:—
5. ómwána‿átémɛ cíɔmbɛ cígēndɛ (B1 as V2)
   The child is to beat the cattle that they go.
6. twábarɔra ábaíbi bágósóa nyomba (A5 as V2)
   We saw the thieves entering the house.
7. ábanto báámɔnyɔɔr(a) okúúre borere (C2/1 as V2)
   People found him dead in bed.
8. bakamɔnyɔɔr(a) ɔréétire‿ébinto bíonsi agacia ka (C2/1 and A4 as V2 and V3)
   They found he had brought all the things and gone off home.
9. nááminyogete akarósa (A4 as V2)
   He ran off and got tired.

Such substitutions might be of the following kind:—
5. ómwána‿átémɛ cíɔmbɛ bɔkóŋu
   The child is to hit the cattle hard.
7. ábanto báámɔnyɔɔra bwango
   People found him quickly.
9. náaminyogete mogondo
   He ran to the fields.

It is clear, however, that while such substitutions may be acceptable for the positions cited, they cannot be accepted as generally valid, since all the tenses concerned may occur in a wide range of other contexts. What is possible, and perhaps profitable, is to consider the range of tenses occurring in a specific position. To note, for example, that in position V2 in Example 5, only Ai2/A1, P1, Px9, Sx1 may replace B1, or in Example 7 that only A4, A5 and P9 may replace C2/1. This is dealt with for each tense in the following examination.
There is, thus, a triple division of tenses, into 'independent' tenses, which may occur in uni-verbal sentences; 'concomitant' tenses, which must occur in multi-verbal sentences, and 'attendant' tenses, which is the term accorded to 'independent' tenses in a multi-verbal context. Finally, a large group of tenses may occur as members of a Substitution-Class.

5.3. From the contexts cited for any particular tense it may be possible to make some sort of generalization at a semantic level. This may be relatively precise in some cases—especially in 'time' tenses—while in others the most that can be done is a rather loose description. The problem is a difficult one, and the linguist must choose between short, often misleading labels, and prolix, but more accurate descriptions based on detailed contextual analysis. To some extent the label may depend on the writer's purpose; it may be argued that for teaching purposes a short label may have advantages over a scientifically more accurate but cumbersome description. In the following pages some generalizations will be proffered wherever possible.

5.4. Substitution tenses. For some tenses it appears possible to substitute other tenses without apparent change of meaning. There are those who will argue—and cogently—that no two shapes can, on principle, ever 'mean the same', and will say that in such examples the differences are, as yet, undiscovered by the linguist. In the present writer's experience it seems truer to say that the ranges of no two tenses are co-extensive, but that in certain cases the use of one tense may be comparable to the use of a second tense, in the sense that it may be possible to substitute that tense for it, with only an 'arguable'[6] distinction in meaning.

2. íg(ɔ) áagenda boBaasi igɔrɔ, agatɛɛba ŋá náírane écum(a) egooca
   He went to boBaasi yesterday and said he would return in a week.
3. ígɔ báagwa nse
   So they fell down.
4. ígɔ kwârúga, goik(a) okarósa
   You cooked until you were exhausted. Cf. A2/1 (i) Ex. 3.
5. íg(ɔ) áaminyoka, goika akarósa
   He ran until he was exhausted.

A2a/1   n-vṕ-á-R-a (Ia -R̄-)   Negative: P2/1, Px2/1 (see below)
Context: Occurs both in uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, and is never associated with the pre-verbal word 'ígɔ' as is A2/1.
Connotation: Time reference as for A2/1. This tense serves to introduce a new topic rather than continue an old one; it is thus complementary, in at least some senses, to A2/1.
Attendant tenses: A2a/1, A2/1, B1, B1a.
Examples:—
1. ngwásíeka(a) égesíeri?
   Did you close the door?
2. ńnágenda asé nágɔtɛɛbia nâré góócia
   I went where I told you I was going.
3. nááminyoka, goik(a) órɔsa
   He ran until he was tired.
4. náátāma tabaisá kóbwatwa
   He ran off in order not to be caught.
5. mwané náákōr(a) érɔngɔɔri nógēndé kónywa?
   At my place I made gruel, will you go and drink it?
6. ndūūsie nkóé ítuko ki? ékiágɛrá nnágor(a) émbori
   What day shall I take it and give it to you? Because I sold the goat. (Reference to the payment of a debt.)
7. ńnákóóra ríírábia kwane rɛɛró níncāāké gósimeka
   I finished clearing at my place, today I'm going to start planting.
Note the occurrence of this tense where preceded by 'onyé' in V1 position, and by 'anga' in V2 position. Negative is Px2/1, q.v.
8. onyé cínyeni ncíátooka, angá ínácigora
   If vegetables were obtainable I would buy them.
9. onyé mbáásímék(a) ékááwa, angá mbáányɔɔr(a) ébitóóki
   If they were to plant coffee they would get a profit.

A2a/2   n-vṕ-a-R-a (IIb -R̄-)   Negative: P2/2, Px2/2
Context: Occurring both in uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, and is never associated with the pre-verbal word 'ígɔ'. Commonly occurs as V1.
Connotation: Time reference as A2/2, and as for A2a/1 tends to introduce a new topic rather than continue one already raised.
Attendant tenses: A2a/2, A2/2, A4, also P5/1.
Examples:—
1. nkwâgenda kwâruusia‿ámagwá mogondo
   You went and removed the thorns from the field.
2. ngwâsíek(a) égesíeri?
   Did you shut the door?
3. ómwák(a) oyó náasíméka kobu(a) ómwák(a) óēta
   This year he planted more than last.
4. náaminyoka, goiká‿akarósa
   He ran until he was exhausted.
5. ómokubio‿óēta‿ábáána mbáagɛnda Gísumu
   Last week the children went to Kisumu.
6. nonyá mwârêént(a) ómorwaír(e) oyo, tákógówɛna
   Although you brought the sick man he won't recover.
As for A2a/1 above, the verbal may be preceded by 'onyé' as V1 and by 'anga' in V2 position.
7. onyé mbáasímék(a) ékááwa, angá mbáanyɔɔr(a) ébitóóki
   If they had planted coffee they would have got a profit.

P2/1   ti-pv́-á(á)-R-a
Context: Commonly occurring in multi-verbal sentences, less commonly in uni-verbal sentences. Commonly in position V2.
Connotation: Past time reference as for A2/1, (i). See also S1.
Attendant tenses: A2/1, A2a/1, D2a/1, P2/1.
Examples:—
1. ńnágɛnd(a) écííro nécímborí‿ibére, tinááɔɔnia nonyá némo
   I went to market with two goats but didn't sell even one.
2. ɛr(ɛ́) óca‿ase twáré kórema, koréndé ncé tináamokwánia
   He came up to where we were cultivating but I didn't greet him.
3. ígɔ nágɛnda mogondo, nágesa‿ake, ómwán(a) óndangeria tináaícóri(a) ékebaari
   I went to the fields and harvested a bit, then my child called me and I didn't fill the bin.
4. ńnaírete cíɔmbɛ ciriá bwéri korénde ticíásɔa
   I took the cattle to the byre but they didn't go in.
Note also the occurrence of this tense preceded by 'angá' usually in position V2. See also Px2/1.
5. onyé ntááika mambia, angá tináámɔrɔra
   If I were not to arrive early I wouldn't see him.

P2/2   ti-vṕ-áa-Ŕ-a (IP)
Context: Commonly occurring in multi-verbal sentences, less commonly in uni-verbal sentences. Usually as V2.
Connotation: Past time reference as A2/2.
Attendant tenses: A2/2, A2a/2, A4.
Examples:—
1. ígɔ nâgɛnd(a) écííro nécímborí‿ibére, tináaɔɔnia nonyá némo
   I went to the market with two goats but didn't even sell one.
2. ígɔ nâirana nyomba, korénde tináamanya gosé‿égesíeri ngésíekíre
   I returned home but did not know that the door was closed.
3. ɛré agataara ncera, korénde ncé tináamokwánia
   He was out walking in the road but I didn't greet him.
4. ígɔ nâgɛnda mogondo nkagɛsa ake, ómwána‿akandangeria tináaícóri(a) ékebaari
   I went to the fields and harvested a bit, then my child called me and I didn't fill the bin.

Px2/1   vp-tá-á-R-a (Ia -R̄-)
This tense occurs in at least three distinct contexts which will here be treated separately.
(i) Context: Multi-verbal sentences only, but may occur as either V1 or V2.
Connotation: Past time reference; generally comparable to A2/1, (i), but there is some looseness.
Concomitant tenses: A2/1, A5, B1, A2/1-Ai1.
Examples:—
1. ínkí rénde ógócia koría ka — ntáárūga?
   What are you going to eat in the house, I didn't cook?
2. totáasíméka marabwóni ébirongó bítíge
   We didn't plant potatoes so that the porcupines would leave them alone. The point here is that in planting potatoes we recognized that the porcupines wouldn't leave them alone, but we persisted nonetheless. Contrast with:—
   égentó kíágɛrɛtɛ titwásíméka‿ámarabwóni nébirongó bíkória
   What caused us not to plant potatoes was the porcupines eating them.
3. totáásaba ngótoa‿árenge bɔ́ɔ́ri?
   We didn't ask, (so) was he really likely to give us (what we wanted)?
4. kái naaré kóruusia nkóe — ntáágor(a) émbori?
   Where could I take (it) and give it to you, as I haven't sold the goat?
5. mwané ntáákōr(a) érɔngɔɔri, nkí ógócia konywá ka?
   At my place I didn't make gruel, what will you eat at home?
6. onyé mbáásímék(a) ébituuma, angá batáaígw(a) éncara
   If they were to plant maize, they would not go hungry.

(ii) Context: Occurs in both uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, and is usually restricted to questions. Occurs commonly as V1.
Connotation: No time reference, but in some contexts with future implication.
Attendant tenses: Px5, D1.
Examples:—
1. ábáána batááriisia buya, nóbokimá bwóká bátagéte bánákória
   The children don't herd well, it is simply food they want—to go on eating.
2. otáásooma otáágóíkaransa!
   As you can't read try sitting down! (Teacher to pupil)
3. otáásooma otáákórema?
   As you can't read will you try farming then?
4. otááráágéra otáákógenda koríísia?
   As you don't eat, will you not go and look after the cattle?
5. náásékí ntáárūga?
   Shall I not cook then? A general question, contrast with:—
   náásékí ntáárūge?
   Shall I not cook?—a particular occasion.
6. ínkí gosé ntáátɛɛbɛri(a) égetɔnga ŋa gósɔria?
   Why should I not tell the basket to play? (From a folk-tale)
7. ŋaí ntáásóóma?
   Where should I not read then?
8. otáátoa ómogaak(a) óyógótóóri(a) ékiina?
   Will you not give us (i.e. please do give us) an elder who can settle the case?

(iii) Context: Multi-verbal sentences only, and those stabilized by n-.
Connotation: Past time reference (see A2/1, (iv)), and may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: A2/1, P5/1, B1, B1a, C2/1.
Substitution Class I.
Examples:—
1. ómonto‿otáákōr(a) émeremo ntááta
   The man who didn't work is my father.
2. ábaíséke bátááca igɔrɔ, ígɔ báatangwa némbura
   The girls who didn't come yesterday were delayed by rain.
3. ábanto bátááírana, ígɔ báásibwa na Bwana D.C.
   The people who didn't return were jailed by the D.C.

Px2/2   vp-tá-a-R-a (Ia -R̄-, IIb -Ŕ-)
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Past time reference comparable to A2/2.
Concomitant tenses: A2/2, A4.
Examples:—
1. ínkí rénd(e) áaca‿akari(a) ka — ntáarūga?
   What did he come and eat in the house, (as) I didn't cook?
2. mwané ntáakōr(a) érɔngɔɔri, nkí kwaré gócia konywá ka?
   In my place I didn't make gruel, what did you go and drink at home?
3. ntáaícóri(a) ényongo igɔrɔ, ékiágɛra nâgɛnda Gɛtɛmbɛ na rɛɛró ímbé tinyícória?
   Yesterday I didn't fill the pot because I went to Kisii and today am I to be that I don't fill it? i.e. Shall I be able to avoid doing it today?
Note the occurrence of the tense in position V1, where preceded by 'onyé, ónyɔ́ɔ́rɛ':—
4. ónyɔ́ɔ́rɛ ntáakóŋa nâkéŋga bɔkóŋu, ígɔ nááre gooca goítwa
   If I hadn't stuck out and defended stoutly I'd have been killed.
5. onyé totáanyɔɔr(a) émeremo Gisumu, angá ígɔ twâgɛnda Nairobi
   If we had not got work in Kisumu we would have gone to Nairobi.

A4   vp-ka-R-a (Ia -â)      Negative: P2/2 q.v.
Context: Restricted to multi-verbal sentences; it may happen, however, in conversation, that a speaker uses the tense in a uni-verbal sentence. In such cases it seems that the speaker is merely continuing his previous utterance. See Example 5 below.
Connotation: Past time reference in relation to the preceding tense. No examples have been recorded of A4 following a future tense.
Concomitant tenses: A2/2, A2a/2, D2/2, D2a/2.
Examples:—
1. botúko ábaíbi bagasóa nyomba, bakaíbâ cíanga, bagasóóka naende

2. níg(ɔ) áácét(e) akaráágéra, éri(ɔ) akaráára
   So he came, ate food and went to sleep.
3. níg(ɔ) áácete, táaráágéra, éri(ɔ) akaráára
   So he came, did not eat and went to sleep.
4. akagɛnda, aga.áák(a) ómwáná goika‿akaréra
   He went and beat the child until it cried.

   B. ɛhɛ́ɛ́ — náaiká?
   A. yááya — akagwá nse agaítwâ kogóro

A5   vp-ko-R-a              Negative: Px2/1-Ai1 (see two-word tenses)
This tense occurs in two distinct kinds of context, with a characteristic tone pattern for vp-ko- in each, which some might regard as justification for treating it as two distinct tenses.
(i) vṕ-kó- for all Classes except 1, 4 and 9, when both prefix and tense sign are low; even in these three Classes both are associated with a high tone if the 'object'[1] of the verbal precedes it.
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences. Commonly occurring in interrogative sentences preceded by an interrogative word.
Connotation: Present time or no-time reference. May be translated by a relative though this is frequently omitted in English.
Attendant tenses: A wide range of such occur, commonly in V2 position, for example: A2/1, P2/1, A5, P5/1, P9, B1a, C2/1, DS/1, D2/1, Px2/1-Ai1.
Substitution Class I: Except where the tense is preceded by an invariable word like 'ékɛrɔ, mbóóno' or by an interrogative word (vide Examples 3, 5 and 6): also, where it is preceded by two short-series nominals (vide Example 7), or may itself have substituted for it tense B1-Ai1 as in Example 10. See also B1-Ai1, p. 62.
Examples:—
1. náámɔrɔra‿ábanto báágósɛka/I saw the people who are laughing.
   .....................ábanénɛ/I saw large people.
   .....................bonsi/I saw all the people.
2. abá tibáámanyetí éki bágókɔra
   They don't know what they are doing.
3. ékɛr(ɔ) ábanto bákógenda koruba, nkónyɔɔra báré cínswi
   When the people go fishing they get fish.
4. námagena‿ákória
   It's eggs he's eating.
5. ŋaí ókógɛnda?
   Where are you going?
6. ndí ókógɛnda?
   When are you going?
7. ómokúngú nécíír(o) ágóócia[2]
   The woman, it is to the market she's going. This is the most common translation for 'The woman is going to market'.
8. námagena‿atááré kórígia
   It's eggs which he's not looking for.
9. mbóóno ig(ɔ) ágóika
   He's just come. Lit.: It is now that he is arriving.
10. ábanto bágóóca, náímóke
   If the people come he'll go.

(ii) vṕ-kó- The tone of vp- is high except in Classes 1, 4 and 9.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only. Usually occurring as V2, but where occurring as V1 seems commonly to be associated with a pre-verbal word 'ígɔ'. (But see Px25/2, Example 1.)
Connotation: No time reference but rather one of continuity. Where the tense occurs as V2, the continuity is associated with the time reference of V1.
Substitution Class: Where this tense occurs immediately following another verbal (Examples 4 and 5) there may be substituted for it an invariable word of 'manner' as in Example 1, or another verbal, C2/1.
Concomitant tenses: A5, A2/1, A2/2, A4, P2/1, P2/2.
Examples:—
1. ....nábarɔra‿ábanto bágɔ̀sɛka/....I saw the people laughing.
   .....................boigɔ/....I saw the people thus.
   .....................baacííre/....I saw the people had gone.
2. tóbá mátúk(o) ábéré tókóaga, éria gatató twâgɛnda ékebósano
   We were two days weeding, on the third day we went all together.
3. íg(ɔ) akógɛnd(a) ékɛr(ɔ) áse ékɛrɔ korɔra gos(e) óbɔri bwarábire
   And he goes from time to time to see whether the millet has ripened.
4. ńnásōa nyomba, nábarɔrá bágókór(a) émeremo
   I entered the house (a few minutes ago) and found them still working.
5. ńnasɔa nyomba, nkabarɔrá bágókóra émeremo
   I entered the house (yesterday) and saw them still working.
6. ígɔrɔ ígɔ nâgɛnda nkórígi(a) émbori (Ai1 preferable here)
   Yesterday I went looking for a goat.
7. ómonto‿osíbéte‿étayi buya, íg(ɔ) akórɔrɛkana buya
   The man who ties his tie well, looks well.

Footnotes:
1. I am defining 'object' rather loosely as any short-series nominal which is in a relationship with a verbal but which does not control the verbal prefix of that verbal. This relationship may be exemplified formally by the presence of an object infix, or it may not.
2. In some speakers the -ko- is realized as -koo- preceding a mono-syllabic radical.

A9   vṕ-raa-R-a
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: Future time reference, commonly with permissive implication. It is also to be noted that the use of this tense commonly implies that another action will subsequently be carried out.
Social context: Commonly in conversation, especially between juniors and their seniors. In its occurrence it may be regarded as evidence of respect accorded by juniors to seniors.
Attendant tenses: B1, B1a.
Examples:—
1. ńdaasóa?
   May I come in?
2. ńdaacá?
   May I come?
3. óraamēntērā cínyeni....
   Will you please give me some more vegetables?....
4. ńdaagósɔria mányé kógɛnda ka
   May I play first, then I'll go home.
5. ńdaakoa nyáŋera yaito, ciaito cínywé mááce
   May I give you our white goat, that our cows may drink water?

P9   ti-vṕ-raa-R-a

Px25/1   vp-tá-á-kó-R-a

Connotation: Time reference future, commonly with an implication of obligation or permission.

Px25/2   vp-tá-á-ko-R-a
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Past time reference, with continuity over a period.
Concomitant tenses: A5, P5/2 (very limited contexts available).
Examples:—

--- 6.2. B Tenses ---
All positive tenses are characterized by Imposed Patterns.

B1   vṕ-Z-R̄-e (Ia -Ŕ-, Ic -CV́CVC-, IIc -CV́CVC-)
This tense occurs in a large number of different contexts, each of which will be treated separately. Commonly referred to as the 'Subjunctive', it is clear that in some cases it may certainly be translated by a form which in turn would be translated by a Latin subjunctive. On the other hand it is equally clear that the range of usage for the two languages is not co-terminous, nor, in terms of its shape, can it be treated in Gusii as anything other than a one-word tense. No labels will here be adopted.

(i) Negative: P1   ti-vṕ-Z-R-a
Note that in the 1st, 2nd and 3rd Persons of Class 2 the vowel of vp- is long (P1), as also in the 2nd and 3rd Persons of Class 1.
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, occurring as V1 and V2.
Connotation: There is an implication of obligation in all contexts.
Attendant tenses: Only a small number of tenses may occur in the position V2, if V1 — being B1 — is not followed by a second SsN controlling new agreements. These tenses are Px9 and B1 or P1. A wider range of tenses occurs in the V1 position where B1 occurs as V2.
Examples:—
1. Bósiborí árɔ̄rɛ ómwáná táágwa korwa bórere
   B. is to see that the child does not fall out of bed.
2. ómoriisia‿átémɛ cíɔmbɛ cígéndɛ
   The herdsboy is to hit the cattle that they go.
3. ábanto tibáárema‿ááŋe na róóce
   People are not to cultivate near the river.
4. taatá agantɛɛbia ŋá tingósória réérɔ
   Father told me I was not to play today.

(ii) Negative: P1   Occurs rarely !
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, occurring commonly as V1, or V2 where V1 is also tense B1. The vp- is commonly in Classes 1/2.
Connotation: No time reference, but rather one of decision to do something at the time of utterance.
Attendant tenses: B1.
Examples:—
1. tógēndɛ tóéésibie róóce
   Let us go and bathe in the river.
2. tíga tósōōme, tómāny(e) ámaŋána
   Let us read that we may know 'things', i.e. become knowledgeable.

(iii) Negative: P1/Ai1. See two-word tenses.
Contexts: Multi-verbal sentences only; occurring as V2.
Connotation: No time reference, but with 'purposive' implication.
Concomitant tenses: A wide range of tenses may occur in the position V1: A2/2, A2a/2, A4, B1, B1a, B9, D2/2.
Examples:—
1. rɛɛró nkógɛndá nde ngōre‿émbori
   Today I'm going to buy a goat.
2. igɔró ńnagenda ngōre‿émbori
   Yesterday I went to buy a goat.
3. ánkio níngéndɛ ngōre‿émbori
   Tomorrow I shall go and buy a goat.
4. mméntɛra cínyéni, nkóórer(e) óbokíma
   Increase for me vegetables so that I may finish the 'porridge'.
5. íg(ɔ) áátáma, tábaisa kóbwatwa
   He ran off so as not to be caught.

(iv) Negative: P1.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only; occurring as V2 after specific radicals. P1 occurs particularly commonly here.
Connotation: This can probably be described only in terms of 'intention'. It is perhaps significant that the tense occurs following such radicals as connote 'forbidding, preventing, wanting'.
Concomitant tenses: Almost all tenses may occur in V1 position, with a few exceptions such as Px25, C1, C2/3.
Examples:—
1. tokamotáng(a) ómoíbi táásóa nyomba
   We prevented the thief from entering the house.
2. nóganetie ónyɔ̄ɔ̄rɛ cíndɛgɛ bun(a) eciigá cíane nayé?
   Do you too want to get bells like these of mine?
3. nkamokáánia táágɛnd(a) écííro
   I forbade him to go to the market.
4. ógacɔgu akab(a) okónágocisab(a) ógasusu ácié gɔtɛnga nacio (Folk tale)
   The elephant was begging the hare to go and dance with them.
5. bagatɛɛma tibáákór(a) émeremo
   They tried not to work.

(v) Negative: P1.
Context: Uni-verbal or multi-verbal sentences following specific radicals.
Connotation: Various.
Concomitant tenses: In V1 position preceding 'goika + B1', commonly B1a. In V2 position following 'goika + B1', commonly B1. A wider range of tenses occurs following or preceding 'mbuya + B1'.
Examples:—
1. goika‿ácé bóóno
   He must come now.
2. nágēndɛ éskuru goika‿ákīīne
   He will go to school until he grows up.
3. mbuya‿áímoke bóóno
   It is better that he leave now.

(vi) Context: Multi-verbal sentences only; occurring as V2 or subsequently.
Connotation: Seems always to be associated with the second (and subsequent) action(s) in a sequence, but not necessarily a sequence in time, for which see A4.
Concomitant tenses: In V1 position, B9 commonly, but not necessarily, occurs. Also A4, P5/1, R1.
Examples:—
1. báátígar(a) ómonyuomi amo nábanto bánde, baké banga banénɛ —bágānye‿ényangi éítwe
   Those who remained were the bridegroom and some other people, young and old, waiting for the marriage to be celebrated (Lit: killed).
2. bagaik(a) égetíí bárɔ̄rɛ iŋ(ɔ) óráác(e) ábue ókominyoka, áīīr(e) ómoíséke....
   They arrived at the clearing to see who would come and win the race and take the girl....
3. ómoriákari táákɔgɛnda bweka átīg(e) ómoísia ncera
   The bride did not go alone and leave the boy in the path.
4. binyinganyingi binge tibiganyiri arɔrɔ babaise gosari(a) enyangi; agwɔ boono monto onde bwensi oraatinde asarie emerooberio yenyangi rirɔrio goika atangwe. (See (v) above.) (No tone-pattern available for this stretch.)
   Many goings and comings are not welcome there, lest they ruin the marriage ceremony; at this time if anyone is drunk he upsets the marriage arrangements, so he must be stopped.
5. Omoiseke igɔ arenge koba as(e) ɔkɔbɔkɔ kwabo goika baroisi(e) amaŋan(a) onsi akɔɛra; bagɛndɛ nomoiseke soobo, batigere soobo‿omoiseke ciɔmbɛ na barabwo baakɔɔcɛrɛra ciɔmbɛ. (No tone-pattern available for this stretch.)
   The girl was in their hands until they had seen to it that all the matters were finished; they went with the girl to her home; waited for the cattle from the girl's father, and accompanied them back.
See also Tense B1a, Example 4.

(vii) There remain to be included here a number of examples which do not conveniently fit elsewhere. They seem to be too few to permit any useful generalization:—
1. gos(e) áb(e) ógosooта
   Perhaps he's reading.
2. noyi(a) óbotok(e) ámagóro kogiica, cíndɛgɛ cíbe. (See also A1a.) From a folk tale.
   Even though the ends of (your) legs are burning, still the bells will materialize. The elephant has risked burning his legs in the belief that he will thus obtain the little bells which the hare has in his possession. This is the hare's taunt!
3. éŋgɔrɔ étɔ́kɔ̃ɔ̃nyɛ tónywe mááce máya!
   May God help us to drink clean water! A common invocation at ceremonies for prosperity.
Examples of this latter pattern, either in uni-verbal or multi-verbal sentences, with B1 occurring as V1 and V2, are not uncommon.

B1a   n(a)-vṕ-Z-R̄-e(IP)   Negative: P5/1   ti-vṕ-kó-R-a. Note: In Class 1, 3rd Person ti-vp realized as táákó-.
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, but not occurring in the latter if B1a occurs in position V1.
Connotation: Future time reference; valid for any future time.
Attendant tenses: A5, A1, B1, B9.
Examples:—
1. níngēndé réérɔ
   I will go today.
   nágēnd(ɛ) ánkio
   He will go tomorrow.
   níngēnd(ɛ) ómwák(a) ógóóca
   I shall go next year.
2. ɔtéébiré nágēnd(ɛ) ánkio
   He has said he would go tomorrow.
3. ɔtéébiré táákógenda‿ánkio
   He has said he would not go tomorrow.

   He said he would go to Kisumu and buy a bicycle and return home.

   N. if he sees M. will hit him.

   Although I'm cultivating this year I'll not harvest.

B9   vṕ-ráá-R̄-e(IP)
This tense occurs in two distinct contexts:
(i) Negative: P5/1 q.v.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only. Where occurring as V1 it is commonly preceded by 'ígɔ', but not when occurring as V2.
Connotation: Future time reference. In some contexts there is an implication of uncertainty.
Concomitant tenses: A5, B1, Sx2/1.
Examples:—
1. ígɔ tóráásōmb(e) ámááce goika áērɛ
   We'll move the water until it is finished. Contrast with B1a in position V1, which introduces the subject as a new one. Here the matter has already been raised.
2. kóndáágwēne nabo ndáárēme
   When I get better I may cultivate.
3. ígɔ ndáámínyoke, tibónyírera
   I'll certainly run, lest darkness falls on me.
4. ígɔ ndáásɔ̄ɛ nyomba, gókóndangeria
   I'll come into the house when you call me.
5. íg(ɔ) óráágēndɛ, ómónyɔ̄ɔ̄ré táraabóóka
   And you'll go and find him not yet awake.

   It (the cow) will finish all the salt which is there.

   It's useless my going, if he doesn't give me any pay.
Note: the occurrence of Sx2/1 rather than Sx1 is unusual.

(ii) Negative: Qx2/3 q.v.
Two features of the verbal prefix should be noted: the tone of the prefix is high, except in Classes 1, 4 and 9. Where the 'object' of the verbal precedes it, the tone of the prefix is high for all Classes. Secondly, as noted in 2.44, the prefix may be associated with an initial vowel.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only, or those stabilized by 'n-'.
Connotation: Time reference future, as for B1a, and may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: A4, B1, B1a, D1a.
Substitution Class I.
Examples:—
1. ómonto‿oráákōrɛ émeremo nómotámbe

3. nóbonyánsi óbo érááriisie. In quick speech this is realized as nóbonyánsi óbwérááriisie
   It is grass which it will feed on. (Reference to a goat.)
4. agatɛɛb(a) ómomura‿áráányuome nori(a) ómuya....

8. bóóno ntórígetié kóbatɛɛma tórɔr(ɛ) óyóráátɔkɔ́ɔ́nyɛ

--- 6.3. C Tenses ---

C1/1   vp-Z-R-íre            Negative: CR1   ti-vṕ-R-iri
As noted in 2.44 the tone of vp- is high except in Classes 1, 4 and 9.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences and those stabilized by n-.
Connotation: No time reference, but rather to a 'state' in existence at the time of utterance. In contrast with verbals characterized by an -w- extension the verbal here is not followed by a SsN which may control the verbal prefix in a passive transformation. The tense may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: A2/2, D2/2, A5, B9.
Substitution Class I.
Examples:—
1. éndááger(a) erúgíre buya....(nengíya)
   Food which is well cooked....(is good).
   Cf. éndáágera (n)érúgegéte buya
   The food is well cooked.
2. óbokímá bórúgíre buya....(ígɔ bórááreegwe bwango)
   Food which is well-cooked....(will be eaten quickly).
3. ébité bínacíre ébiéŋɛ....
   Twigs which are cut small....
4. oyó nómonto‿óyotáírókiri
   He is a man who is not feared.
5. cíɔmbɛ ticíírókiri....ciasírire
   The cattle which are not feared....have got lost.
6. ómonto‿óyosibíre ntaatá
   The man who is tied up is my father.
   Cf. ómonto‿oyó_osíbire
   The man who has tied up....(vide C2/1).
7. ómonto‿ociikír(e) ágēndɛ....
   The man who is ordered to go....
   Cf. ómonto‿oncííkire ngēndɛ
   The man has ordered me to go.
8. óbɔri óbo bóbusuíre buya....(ígɔ bóráárabe buya)
   Millet which is well planted....(will ripen quickly).
   Cf. óbɔri obó‿óbobusuíre buya....
   This millet which is well planted....
9. émerem(o) eyé íg(ɔ) ékóríre buya....
   The work which is well done....
   Cf. émerem(o) eyé yakórirwe búya
   This work has been well done.
10. ómonto‿oyó_oírókíre mono....(obaítir(e) ábanto bábére)
   This man, who is widely feared....has killed two people.
   Cf. ómonto‿óyoirókire monɔ
   The man who is very fearful.
11. ás(e) ómotw(e) oyé abwate énungu éngiy(a) ébaacíre buya
   On his head he has a fine stick, which is well sharpened.
12. ....korw(a) émiák(a) eyió ériikíre igoro....
   ....from those years which are written of above....
13. ....ókorundarunda áse‿émeté‿ékorekanírie bɔkóŋu némɛgóyɛ
   ....pushing backwards and forwards where the trees are tightly interlaced with bark-rope.

C1/2   vp̄-Z-R̄-ire           Negative: CR1/2   ti-vṕ-R̀-irí (IP?) (rare!)
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: As C1/1, but is not translated by a relative.
Attendant tenses: A2/1, A2/2, D2/1, D2/2.
Examples:—
1. éndááger(a) eyé mbuy(a) ērūgire
   This food is well cooked.
   Cf. C1/1. In sentences of this pattern where the verbal is preceded by a piece with initial n-, its distribution is clearly complementary to C1a.
2. ....érɔngɔɔrí yáinekwa nécinyongo cinénɛ yába ēīnkīr(e) ámatúk(o) átáto
   ....the gruel was placed in big pots and was placed there for three days.
3. ábanto bágócia koígwa, bátɛɛb(a) ékiina tikénaciri buna ámaŋana ámanyéné táraaɛ́ra
   When the people go and hear, they say the case is not decided, as the words themselves have not finished yet.
4. noonyá nékɛr(ɔ) ákwété tárokirí mwáná ríétá ríaye
   Even when he died there was not called a child by his name.

R1   ti-vṕ-R-ire   Only one example of this tense has been recorded!
Context: Multi-verbal sentence.
Connotation: As C1/2.
Concomitant tenses: D2/2, A4.
Example:—
1. ékɛrɔ báaciéte gotindokor(a) óboin(a) obwo bwoye, bakanyɔɔra Sákaawa tátíndékire árɔrɔ
   When they went to uncover his tomb, they found that Sakaawa was not buried there.

C1a   n-vṕ-Z-R-ire (I -írē, II -ire)   Negative: CR1/2
Context: Appears to be restricted to uni-verbal sentences, and to a rather narrow range of radicals.[1]
Connotation: As C1/2.
Examples:—
1. ómonto‿oyó námanyírē buya
   This man is well known (by name and appearance).
2. ábant(o) abá mbáncírē mónɔ
   These folk are well liked.
3. tósóa nyomba, ómwána nárááire
   Don't go into the house, the child is asleep.
4. ómonto‿oyó náírókírē mónɔ
   This man is widely feared. Cf. C1/1, Example 10.
5. ómwán(a) oyó náteneínē gesíéri
   This child is standing by the door.
   Contrast with—
   ómwán(a) oyó ngesíéri ateneíne (C1/1)
   This child, it is by the door where he's standing.
6. éndááger(a) eyé nérúgírē buya
   This food is cooked well. Cf. C1/2.

Footnote:
1. The radicals most commonly exemplified in this tense are the following:— -anc-/Like, -many-/Get to know, -tenen-/Stand, -raar-/Go to sleep, -irok-/Fear, -rɔr-/See. By analogy with other radicals it seems possible that the eccentric shape '-rooce' can most usefully be considered as an additional C form—since '-rɔrire' also occurs. In Tense C1 there is a 'figurative' connotation, with general time reference, 'Ndooc(e) ómot(e) oyó‿oisire kogwa'/I see the tree is about to fall. In Tense C1a, by contrast, there is a connotation of 'literal' seeing, often with a present time connotation, 'Níndooc(e) ómot(e) oyó'/I see this tree. A negative shape, 'ti-vp-rooci', also occurs.

C2/1   vp-a-Ŕ-ire (Ia -íre, Ic -CV́CVC-, IIc -CV́CVC-)   Negative: P9, Px9, Sx2/1
This tense occurs in two distinct contexts.
(i) Negative: P9
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: No time reference but rather to a state of affairs existing at the time of utterance, resultant upon some action undertaken in the past and since completed.
Attendant tenses: A4, P5/1, B1, B1a.
Examples:—
1. ómwán(a) ocíire‿écííro
   The child has gone to market.
2. éŋɔmbɛ eyé yabíarire émɔɔri émwámu
   This cow has given birth to a black calf.
3. ocíire rooc(e) ábūūc(e) ámááce
   She has gone to the river to draw water.
4. akamɔnyɔɔr(a) okúúre
   He found him dead (lit: he has died).
5. noonyá mwaréétir(e) ómorwaire, táákógwena
   Though you brought the sick man he will not recover.
6. ocíire rooce korénde náírane bwango
   She has gone to the river but will return soon.
7. ómwán(a) oné nágēndɛ éskuru goik(a) obéir(e) ómonto ómɔnénɛ
   My child will go to school until he's a grown man.

(ii) Negative: Px9, Sx2/1
Context: Multi-verbal sentences and those stabilized by n-.
Connotation: As C2/1, but may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: P5/1, B1a, B1, C2/1, D2/1.
Substitution Class I.
Examples:—
1. ómonto‿ocíre rɛɛrɔ nómɔgɛni one
   The man who has come today is my guest.
2. órobágo‿órwɔ órwagúúre igɔ rwágítetwe noyó minto ómɔnénɛ
   The fence which has fallen down was put up by my elder brother.

C2/2   vṕ-á-R-ire (IIa -íre)   Negative: CRx2/1   vp-tá-á-R-íre
Two points relating to the tone-patterns of these tenses should be noted:—
(a) C2/2. Low-toned radicals only. In Classes 1, 4 and 9 the radical is low-toned, and in all other Classes the radical has a high tone which doubles on to the following tone of the '-ire' where this is low, and is realized as a mid-tone, thus:—
ómogak(a) ógéécīre (nómokóro)/émegaka yágɛɛcire (némekóro)
The 'omogaka' tree(s) which has/have been cut down (is/are old)
émbori yásibire (yatámire)/címbori cíasíbíre (ciatámire)
The goat(s) which was (were) tethered (has/have run off).
(b) CRx2/1. The tone of the verbal prefix in Classes 1, 4 and 9 only, is low-toned. This occurs for all radicals.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only or those stabilized by n-.
Connotation: Past time reference with a 'stative' implication as for C1/1. The tense may be translated by a relative in English.
Concomitant tenses: A2/1, C2/1, D2/1.
Substitution Class I.
Examples:—
1. ámaŋana‿áámanyíre nábagááka, bóóno eebírwe
   Words which were known by the leaders are now forgotten.
2. ábáána‿ábange báácííkire báténene, bákire, bóóno baamínyokire
   Many children who were told to stand still have now run off.
3. cíɔmbɛ cítáásibire ciatámire
   The cattle which were not tethered have run off.
4. ékɛr(ɔ) ómont(o) abétirwe ríítumo gos(e) énsara gos(e) ómogwe bíáákir(e) óbosongo‿obwɔ, táárenge kogwena
   When a man has been wounded with a spear or an arrow tipped with iron or wood, and these have been poisoned, then he doesn't get better.

C2/3   vṕ-â-R-íre (IP)       Negative: CRx2/2   vp-tá-a-Ŕ-íre (IP) (Ic -CV́CVC-, IIc -CV́CVC-)
Note: Tense C2a/3, with a non-relative connotation, has been noted very rarely. It should also be noted that in CRx2/2 the verbal prefix is low-toned in Classes 1, 4 and 9 only. This has not been observed for C2/3.

8. éndááger(a) etáarūgíre yagúndire

4. tináakáɔɔnire‿ébituuma biane kérɔ kiabóóno
   I've never sold my maize at a time like this (with low prices prevailing).

Rx24/1   vp-tá-ká-R-ire
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Recent past time reference, with implication of continuity.
Concomitant tenses: A5, C2, A9, Ai1/A1.
Examples:—
1. ntágásibire‿émborí yane — inkí rénde náare gɔkɔ́ra — bóóno yamánoire
   I was not in the habit of tying my goat and then, what was I doing, now it has moved off.
2. ntágásiire korwa mambia, bóóno bwakíire — nkógɛnda nde mógondo
   Since this morning I've not ground, now time has passed — I'm off to the fields.
3. ntákángire korema korwa mambia, mbóóno ngóóca kwanga tíndema konaísire gotábókia?
   Since morning I've not refused to cultivate, now am I going to refuse when I've almost reached the end?
4. ntákáɔɔnire‿ébituumá bíane korwa mambia, bóóno tiga ndaabíírania gocia ka
   I've not sold any of my maize since the morning, now I'll take it back home.

Rx24/2   vp-tá-a-ka-R-ire
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Time reference less recent, with implication of continuity.
Concomitant tenses: A5, A975, B1a.
Examples:—
1. ntáagasibire mborí cíane, nacínār(e) ógosibwa bɔɔrí?
   I was not in the habit of tying my goats, will they really get used to being tied?
2. ntáagasiire, néreego ncé ndaanákóíra
   I didn't do any grinding, it is to the mill I'll continue to take (it).
Note: the positive version of this is as follows:—
   ígɔ naagasíire, ncé tínkóíra‿ase‿éreego!
   I've been grinding, I shan't send it to the mill!
   It is not clear why CR24/2 cannot occur in the negative version!
3. ntáakaangire korema koru (korwa) kare, mbóóno ngóóca kwangá tíndema?
   I've never refused to cultivate since long ago, is it now that I'm coming to refuse to cultivate?

C29   vp-a-ráá-R̄-īre
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: Time reference recent past, with the implication of doing something subsequent to the action already carried out. See other '9' tenses.
Attendant tenses: Px2/2, P1/Ai1.
Examples:—
1. ndóócé mwatáárire! éé, twaráátāārīre
   I see you've been walking! Yes, we've been for a stroll.
2. twaráásibīrie cíanga cirocio, íírema totáarema
   We've finished washing the clothes, but the cultivation we haven't done yet.
3. naráásīkir(e) ábáána‿abá‿ámatúk(o) ámange ko, tibárí kónyígwéra
   I've been respecting these children for many days but they don't listen to me.
4. naráárɔrir(e) ábanto — bátáí báke!
   I've seen people — not a few! A statement of worldliness!


--- 6.4. D Tenses ---

D1   vṕ-Z-R-ete (IIa -Ŕ-)
This tense occurs in two main contexts:—
(i) Here it should be noted that the tone of vp- in Classes 1, 4 and 9 is low, with final -éte. In all other Classes vṕ- is high, and followed by final -eté.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences, and those stabilized by n-.
Connotation: General time reference; may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: A5, P1, P1/Ai1, B1, Sx1.
Substitution Class I.
Examples:—
1. ómonto‿osíbéte‿étayi buya, íg(ɔ) akórɔrɛkana buya
   The man who ties his tie well looks smart.
2. ábáána básoomete, nábaké kobua ábatásoometi
   The children who read are fewer than those who do not (i.e. go to school).
3. ábáána báganetié gósooma, nábange kobua cískuru
   The children who want to read are more numerous than the schools.
4. ényomb(a) eyio, nómwán(a) oyé ésinyéte
   This house, it is (towards) his son (which) it faces.
5. ŋaí óményétɛ? naó ményét(ɛ) aiga
   Where do you live? I live here (lit: it is here that I live here).
6. intwé tómɛnyɛt(ɛ) aiga nábaGusii
   We who live here are Gusii.
   This is barely acceptable, the common version being:—
   intwé ábatóményɛtɛ....
7. ékɛrɔ tósíbeté címbori, ticírí kória ndáágera‿ánde
   When we fasten up the goats they don't eat food elsewhere.
8. ékɛrɔ ncaagét(e) órɔgɛndɔ, tíndí gotenena‿ánde
   When I go on a journey I don't stop anywhere.

(ii) Here the vp- appears to be high for all Classes, with final -éte.
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: General time reference; not translated by a relative.
Attendant tenses: A5, P1/Ai1.
Examples:—
1. ényomb(á)esinyéte bwóye
   The house faces towards his place.
2. noonyá ncé mmanyéte koroosi(a) égɛtɔnga
   Even I know how to weave a basket.

D1a   n-vṕ-Z-R-ete          Negative: S1 q.v.
This tense is restricted in its occurrence to certain radicals. -anc-/Like, -gani-/Hope for, -ikarans-/Sit down, and -tenen-/Stand, occur most commonly.
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: General time reference.
Attendant tenses: Only B1 and P1 recorded.
Examples:—
1. ábáána mbááncete‿ébint(o) ébiyi‿a
   Children like new things.
2. ómonto‿oyó náganeti(e) ányɔ̄ɔ̄rɛ ényange
   This man hopes to get a bicycle.

S1   ti-vṕ-Z-R-eti
This tense occurs in two distinct contexts:—
(i) Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, but restricted to those radicals for which D1a occurs.
Connotation: General time reference.
Attendant tenses: A5, D1.
Examples:—
1. titómanyeti as(e) áményétɛ
   We do not know where he lives.
2. timbáánceti ábanto bágókwán(a) óborimo
   I do not like people who tell lies.

(ii) Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: Recent past time reference. See both A2/1 and D2/1.
Attendant tenses: A2/1, D2/1 (A2a/1, D2a/1).
Examples:—
1. rɛɛró égetáábu kiane tigétandogéti
   Today my book didn't tear (lit: split).
2. ígɔ áágendétɛ Bósongo kórendé táíraneti
   So he went to Kisii but did not return.
3. tínyébeti égetáábú keria ántɛɛbétie
   I didn't forget the book he asked me about.
4. igɔró ómwán(a) oriá táréétɛti cínyeni cíyio
   Yesterday the child did not bring (me) those vegetables.
5. intwé titógɛndɛti Bósongo, ékiágɛr(a) émbura njátwa
   We didn't go to Kisii because it rained.

Sx1/1   vp-tá-Z-R-eti
Although two distinct contexts have been recorded for this tense, the first is by far the commoner:—
(i) Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Future time reference, with conditional implication. See two-word tenses, B1(-is-)/Ai1.
Concomitant tenses: P1, P5/1, B1, B1a.
Examples:—
1. otákórɛti émeremo, nóáākwe
   If you do not work you will be beaten.
2. batásímégeti ébituuma, tibákónyɔɔr(a) éndáágera
   If they do not plant maize they will not get any food.
3. émbur(a) etátweti, nátōbe néncara
   If it doesn't rain we shall go hungry.
4. tínkórúga otááŋeeti óbosie
   I won't cook unless you give me flour.
5. ómosáác(a) otámɛrɛti cíndɛru, tárí buya átōre mokáaye botúko kágóóca, tig(a) ókɔŋɔnta gesíéri rióka
   A man if he hasn't got a beard (lit: grow face-hairs), it is not good that he wake his wife at night when he comes; better that he knock at the door only.

(ii) Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: General time reference, and may be translated by a relative in English. See D1 (i).
Concomitant tenses: D1 only recorded.
Substitution Class I.
Example:—
1. ábáána básoomete, nábake kobua ábatásoometi
   The children who read are fewer than those who do not.

Sx1/2   vp-tá-a-R-eti
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Past time reference, with conditional implication.
Concomitant tenses: A2/2, D2/2.
Examples:—
1. onyé totáasímégetí ámaɛmba, angá éncará njatoria
   If we had not planted sorghum, we would have gone hungry (lit: hunger would have eaten us).

D2/1   vṕ-á-R-ete
This tense occurs in two distinct contexts:—
(i) Negative: DS2/1   ti-vṕ-á-R-éte
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only. Commonly preceded by 'ígɔ' where occurring as V1.
Connotation: Time reference recent past, that is to say any time from about 1 to 12 hours preceding the time of utterance, but there is some looseness, particularly among the younger generations.
Concomitant tenses: C2/1, A2/1, D2/1.
Examples:—
1. Bwána D.C. náruusia‿ábanto rííaaki ígɔrɔ, ékiágɛra bááremete cínyeni aaŋe ná róóce
   The D.C. fined (some) people yesterday for planting vegetables near the river.
2. órobágo‿órwɔ orwagúúre ígɔ rwágítetwe noyó mínto ómɔnénɛ
   The fence that has fallen down was built by my elder brother.
3. íg(ɔ) áátɛɛbetɛ ŋ(a) ócié gósímék(a) ébiási aariá ríiranya
   And he said, Go and plant potatoes over there near the river.
4. ígɔ twarááire ncara, tináárúgete — nyéni ticíáre nááre korugera
   So we slept on hunger, as I didn't cook, there being no vegetables to cook with (lit: there were no vegetables I was cooking with).
5. ábaíbi mbáácet(e) áiga, nkwábaígwéte? Yááya tináábaígwéte
   Thieves came here, did you hear them? No, I didn't (hear them).

(ii) Negative: Sx2/1
vṕ- in all Classes except Classes 1, 4 and 9 in high-toned radicals. For low-toned radicals see 2.44.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only, or sentences stabilized by n-.
Connotation: As (i), but may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: D2/1, Sx2/1, P9, C2/1.
Substitution Class I.
Examples:—
1. ábáána báágánetie gosooma, nábángé bááre kobua cískuru
   The children who wanted to learn were more numerous than the schools.
2. ábaíbi báásɔ́ɛtɛ nyomba botúko, ígɔ báaíbete‿ébint(o) ébínge
   Thieves who entered the house during the night stole many things.
3. ómɔgɛni ocete rɛɛrɔ, nNáírobí árwéte
   The stranger who came today came from Nairobi.
4. ómonto ɔkórɛtɛ émeremo, íg(ɔ) áreng(e) ómotámbe
   The man who worked was tall.
5. ígɔ báábeete buna nábanto báásamberetwe‿ébiránya bikabaŋusa gooca‿áiga
   They were like people who had had magic burned against them. It drew them on here.

D2a/1   n-vṕ-á-R-ete[1]      Negative: DS2/1, Sx2/1 q.v.
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, where it tends to occur as V1.
Connotation: Past time reference as for D2/1.
Attendant tenses: D2/1, A4, B1.
Examples:—
1. náácéte? eé, náácete botúko
   Did he come? Yes, he came during the night.
2. náákóórete korééta‿ámááce?
   Did he finish bringing water?
3. náásímégete‿ébituumá mógondo
   He planted maize in the field(s).
4. mbáacete bakandéétɛr(a) ékɛ́ɛ́gwa
   They came and brought me a present.
5. náágésetɛ mambia, naendé náác(e) áíran(e) ánkio?
   He harvested this morning, is he going to return tomorrow?
Note also the occurrence of the tense preceded by 'onyé' commonly in position V1. See also Px2/1.
6. onyé náácaagete‿émeremo kemambia, angá náákoora kebwángo
   If he were to start work early, he would finish quickly.

Footnote:
1. There is evidence that D2a/1 may be associated—albeit not commonly—with a negative connotation, where it is associated with the negative tone-pattern, but not with the negative tense sign:— ntwárúgéte/We didn't cook (this morning, recently). The evidence regarding D2a/2 is conflicting.

Sx2/1   vp-tá-á-R-éte (IIa -ete)
This tense occurs in two distinct contexts:—
(i) Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Recent past time reference.
Concomitant tenses: A5, B1, D1.
Examples:—
1. ntáárúgéte bokímá rééró mwane, nkí bágóóca koria?
   I didn't cook food today, what are they going to eat?
2. atáágésétɛ mambia, na bóóno ábe táácia kɔgésa?
   He didn't harvest this morning, now how can he not harvest? (lit: how is he to be that he is not to go and harvest?).
3. ntáákóóréte ríírabia kwane, na rɛɛró ngócia
   I didn't finish clearing at my 'farm', today it is I'm going there.
4. ómokúngu óyosóógéte cíɔmbɛ cigaikeerana, atáábaséte ómosáác(a) oy(e) ógókwa
   A woman who is married, and the cows are paid up, did not run off (with another man) if her husband died.
5. ntáákáméte éŋɔmbɛ mambia, nnakí kégóóca koba?
   I didn't milk the cows this morning, what's going to happen?

(ii) The tone of the verbal prefix is high except for Classes 1, 4 and 9.
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: As (i), but may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: A2/1, P2/1, D2/1 (A2a/1, D2a/1).
Substitution Class I.
Examples:—
1. ómonto‿otáákórétɛ émeremo, íg(ɔ) áreng(e) ómotámbe
   The man who didn't work was tall.
2. ábanto bátáámanyéte gosooma, nábasénci
   People who didn't know to read were uncivilized.
3. ábanto bátááígweréte, ígɔ bááruusetigw(e) óboaini
   The people who didn't obey orders were fined.

D2/2   vṕ-a-R-éte (Ia both -Ŕ- and -R̀- have been recorded)
As in the case of D2/1, this tense occurs in two distinct contexts:—
(i) Negative: DS2/2
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, in which latter commonly occurring as V2.
Connotation: Time reference less recent past; commonly occurring for time beyond two or three days ago.
Attendant tenses: D2a/2.
Examples:—
1. náaciéte kɔrɔra‿ááse kwâreméte gos(e) atóbire
   He went to see the place you cultivated, whether it was fertile.
2. áse éngɛɛcɔ yókomoc(a) émbura, ígɔ báaroosétie émecaacaakwa
   Because of a shortage of rain they constructed ditches.
3. éndáágera nábwaté nénke; yaɛ́rire ékiágera tináagésétɛ éndááger(a) ényinge
   The food I have is a little; it is finished because I didn't harvest much food.
4. tináasímégéte‿ébituuma‿ás(e) ómogondo‿one
   I didn't plant maize in my 'farm'.

(ii) Negative: Sx2/2
Context: Multi-verbal sentences, and those stabilized by n-.
Connotation: Time reference as for (i), but may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: A2/2, P2/2, D2/2, DS2/2.
Substitution Class I.
Example:—
1. ábáána báaganétie gosooma, nábánge baaré kóbua cískuru
   The children who wanted to learn were more numerous than the schools.

D2a/2   n-vṕ-a-R-éte (Ia -Ŕ- or -R̀-)   Negative: DS2/2
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: Time reference less recent past, as D2/2.
Attendant tenses: A4, D2/2.
Examples:—
1. nnâgɛndét(ɛ) áse nnâgɔtɛɛbétie
   I went where I told you I was going.
2. mbáacéte bakandéétɛr(a) ékɛ́ɛ́gwa
   They came and brought me a present.
3. nnâkwerétwe ékɛrɔ naaréng(e) ómwána
   I was bereaved when I was a child.
Note also the occurrence of this tense preceded by 'onyé', commonly in position V1:—
4. onyé náacaagéte‿émeremo kémambia, angá‿atáákɔ̃r(a) émeremo bóóno
   If he had started work early, he would not be working now.

Sx2/2   vp-tá-a-R̀-éte
Like Sx2/1, this tense occurs in two distinct contexts:—
(i) Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Less recent past time reference, as for D2/2.
Concomitant tenses: A5, B1, B9.
Examples:—
1. ómwák(a) oyó atáasímégéte‿ébituuma, nkí ábááná báráárie?
   This year he didn't plant maize, what will the children eat?
2. atáaniibéte ŋɔmbɛ, nkí réndé mókória?
   He didn't go in for owning cattle, what are you going to eat?
   (Comment by a woman on her late husband, to recently arrived guests.)

(ii) Context: Multi-verbal sentences only. Verbal prefix tone is high, except in Classes 1, 4 and 9.
Connotation: As D2/2, but may be translated by a relative.
Concomitant tenses: A4, D2/2.
Substitution Class I.
Example:—
1. ábaíbi báasɔétɛ nyomb(a) ómwák(a) ôéta, ígɔ báaíbéte ébint(o) ébínge
   The thieves who entered the house last year stole many things.

D4   vṕ-ká-R-éte          Negative: DS4   ti-vṕ-ká-R̀-ete (IP)
Context: Uni-verbal (rarely) and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: General past time reference, with an implication of distance from the speaker and context of utterance.
Attendant tenses: A2/2, A2/1-Ai1, A5.
Examples:—
1. kórendé nyancá bákáményétɛ nábant(o) ábángé baGusii
   But on the lake they were living with many Gusii people.
2. ígɔ baaré kómobwáter(a) émbori, báamanya ŋa nɛɛré ógácɛgétɛ
   And when they caught the goat they realized that it was a troublesome beast.
3. kéra‿ábáána igɔ báárenge korígia komanya ábaŋinamoke, asé bágásóógéte
   Each of the children was looking for the place their aunts had come from.
4. cíndééria ciabó ás(e) ábaŋina bákárwéte
   Their relatives in the place whence their mothers came.
5. ás(e) ómoigw(a) oyé ágásóógéte ómoirera‿ámarwa
   To the place where his sister's child was married from, thence she sent beer to him.
6. ómwánɛw(a) ógɛnda gosangereria círɔngɔɔri korwa asé bákábéégéte, ókórénta‿áás(e) ámo
   The favourite went and collected gruel from where they had placed it, and took it to one place.
7. ábaŋina‿ábanyuomi bákárwéte cínseem(o) eció nació baaré kórómia
   The mothers of the suitors, it is to the place they came from that they make them (the suitors) look.

D4a   n-vṕ-ká-R-éte         Negative: DS4
Context: Uni-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: General past time reference, with an implication of distance from the place of utterance.
Examples:—
1. níngákóóréte!
   I've finished it (somewhere else)!
2. nógákáméte éŋɔmbɛ? eé, níngákáméte
   Have you milked the cow? Yes, I have.

D24/1   vṕ-á-ká-R-éte (C1.1, 1st Pers. nááká-)
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences, commonly preceded by the invariable word 'mbóno'.
Connotation: Recent past time reference, with some implication of emphasis.
Attendant tenses: Px9, B1 and C2/1.
Examples:—
1. mbóno náákárúgéte!
   Certainly I've finished cooking!
2. mbóno náágákóóréte‿émeremo botáraaíra
   Certainly I've not yet finished the work and it is not yet dark.
3. mbóno gwágácíéte?
   Haven't you gone yet?
4. mbóno gwákárúgéte?
   Haven't you done the cooking yet? (Implying that she surely ought to have finished long ago.)
5. agatɛɛbia maasamo, Baabá ígɔ ákáganétie cíntuga monɔ!
   She said to the hyena, Mother's been longing for flying ants! (From a folk tale.)

D24/2   vṕ-a-ka-R-éte
Context: As for D24/1.
Connotation: Less recent past time reference; commonly preceded by the invariable word 'mbóno'.
Attendant tenses: Px9.
Example:—
1. mbóno náagakóóréte‿émeremo botáraaíra
   Indeed I finished the work before it got dark.

D29a/1   n-vṕ-á-raa-R̀-éte (IP)
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: Time reference recent past, with the implication of a subsequent action to follow that in question.
Concomitant tenses: A2/1, Px9.
Examples:—
1. nnáraasibéte címbori, námanya kɔgɛnda koriisia cíɔmbɛ
   I tethered the goats first and then went and herded the cattle.
   Cf. ńnásiba címbori go, tináácia cíɔmbɛ
   I tethered the goats but I didn't go and herd the cattle.
2. náraakáánéte ríítááŋani Músiona‿atáraaca
   He first refused, prior to Musiona's arrival.
3. ntwáraabuucét(e) ámáác(e) atáraasareka
   We drew water before it got dirty.

D29a/2   n-vṕ-á-ráa-R̀-éte (IP)
Context: Multi-verbal sentences only.
Connotation: As for D29a/1 but time reference less recent.
Concomitant tenses: A4, Px9.
Examples:—
1. nnáráasibéte cíŋɔndi, nkamanya góóca
   I tied up the sheep and then came.
2. ábaíséke mbáráabuucét(e) ámááce, bakamanya gocia gɔtéénya
   The girls drew water and then went to cut firewood.
3. mbáráaganyét(e) ake, bakamanya kɔgɛnda
   They waited a bit and then went on.
4. ntwáráabuucét(e) ámáác(e) atáraasareka
   We drew water before it got dirty.


=== 7.0. TWO-WORD TENSES ===

7.1. In which the radical is -re, commonly with extension -nge (vide passim).

Ai2/A1   nkó-R-a vṕ-re (Ia, IIa -R̀-)   Negative: P1/Ai1, Px1/Ai1 (q.v.)
Context: Uni-verbal and multi-verbal sentences.
Connotation: Present, or more rarely, general time reference. Continuity or, more rarely, habituity seem to be implied.
Attendant tenses: B1a, C2/1, A2/1, A5, Ai2-A2/1.
Examples:—
1. nkógɛnda ńde bóóno korígi(a) émbori
   Now I'm going looking for the goat.
2. ngókora‿áre‿émeremo kera ríítúko
   He works every day.
3. nkórema nááre bóóno ngótimoka nde
   I've been hoeing and now I'm resting.
4. ékɛr(ɔ) ábáána baabóókire, nígɔ ngókɔra báre‿émeremo
   When the children awake, they work (i.e. make a habit or point of so doing).
5. ékɛrɔ nkómɔrɔra, ngókɔra‿áre‿émeremo
   When I see him he's working (i.e. was before I saw him).
6. ékɛrɔ námɔrɔra, ngókɔra‿árenge émeremo

=== 9.0. CONCLUSIONS ===

9.0. Conclusions:
From the evidence presented above it seems profitable to summarize what appear to be the main features of the system, and then endeavour to assess the relationship of Gusii to other broadly similar languages of Zone E (Gĩkũyũ, Kuria, Nyore, (nya-)Nkore)[1] by arbitrarily selecting for comparison a number of those features which appear to be characteristic of Gusii.[2]
The Gusii tense system is characterized by four post-radical suffixes (-a, -e, -ire, -ete (see 1.12 for realization of final vowels)), and a relatively small number of tense signs, which may, however, combine one with another. Thus 2 may be followed by 4, 5, 75 and 9; 4 by 75, and 9 by 75. 7, which always occurs in association with 5, always occurs immediately preceding the radical (or an object-infix where occurring), while 4 and 5 appear to be mutually exclusive. A number of tenses with initial n- occur; with one exception these are all positive tenses. These n- tenses are of two kinds; those which are closely related semantically to similar tenses in which an initial n- does not occur (cf. A2/1, A2a/1; D2/1, D2a/1), and those in which there is no such relationship (cf. A1 and A1a). The former will be referred to as Type 1, the latter as Type 2. There are two negative signs, ti- and -tá-, of which the latter commonly, but by no means invariably, occurs in multi-verbal sentences. Negative tenses have been here defined as those characterized by one or other of these signs (but see fn. p. 53). In many cases these negative tenses cannot be related by shape or meaning to positive tenses.[3] (See P1, P5/1, qx2/1.) In the differentiation of past and future time, the system is asymmetrical, there being four divisions of past time and only one of future time. A considerable number of tenses are distinguished only by tone-pattern, and a characteristic pattern occurs in the verbal prefix of Classes 1, 4 and 9 in those tenses which may be translated by a relative. In 'direct' relative sentences, where a preceding SsN controls the verbal prefix, both the shape of that prefix and the tone-pattern are characteristic. In 'indirect' relative sentences where the 'object' of the relative verbal precedes it, a pre-verbal item occurs, differing only in tone-pattern from the Demonstrative (1).[4]

One of the difficulties of making comparisons between features of different systems, is that of establishing the comparability of such features. This is particularly so where, for example, a lack of tonal data makes identification difficult, or where a lack of contextual data precludes any semantic inferences. Semantic criteria in any case raise special problems of their own, well exemplified by the negative sign -tá-, which appears to occur in all the four languages noted above, including Gusii. In at least one instance in Kuria, for example, and possibly also in Gusii (see Px25), it is not possible to ascribe a negative meaning to the sign, so that one is tempted to regard it as different from that associated with a negative meaning. Yet to do this means imposing an alien semantic structure on the facts; if one eschews labelling tenses by their translation, one ought surely to extend the principle to elements. If they appear to be formally comparable then this must form the primary data for comparison, and observations about meaning must take second place, though this does not mean that meaning should not be subjected to a most careful and detailed analysis. It may well be that one of the distinguishing features of such languages is the extent to which apparently comparable shapes are associated with quite different meanings (i.e. Gusii: narúgire/I have cooked; Kuria: narúgire/I cooked (yesterday or earlier).[5]

Turning now to some of the related languages mentioned above, there is in Gĩkũyũ a five-fold division of post-radical suffix, including -aga. A larger number of tense signs occurs, including -ra-, -rĩ-, -kĩ-, -ro- and -ngo-, not occurring in Gusii, but there is much less evidence of tense-sign combination. Tenses with initial nĩ- of Type 1 occur. Two negative signs occur as in Gusii but both occur as infixes.[6] There is a symmetrical division of time into three past and three future tenses. While Barlow does not record any tense differentiated solely by tone-pattern,[7] there is evidence that particular patterns occur where the controlling SsN is of Classes 1, 4 or 9. Of tenses which may be translated by a relative, it has been said, '...the most important factor in relative constructions is the tonal pattern of the relative verbal, this is shown by the fact that the head-word may be omitted, with modification of meaning but not of relative signification'.[8]

In Nyore (Luyia) there is a four-fold division of post-radical suffix; including -nga but excluding -VtV. Again there is a small number of tense signs with little evidence of tense-sign combination. Tenses with initial ni- occur, both of Type 1 and 2. Two negative signs occur (shi-, -ra-), the former as a prefix, the latter as an infix. Tenses distinguished by tone have not been recorded. In tenses which may be translated by a relative, the most important feature seems to be the shape of the verbal prefix. Division of time is symmetrical, with four future and four past tenses.

Only three post-radical suffixes appear to occur in (nya-)Nkore, and there are a relatively small number of tense signs; no evidence is available for any tense-sign combination. A tense of Type 2 with initial ni- occurs. Negative tense signs as for Gusii occur, but no tonal data is available, and no examples of tenses distinguished only by tone-pattern have been recorded. Differentiation of past and future time is asymmetrical, there being three divisions of past time and two of future time. As with Nyore, in direct relative sentences it is the shape of the verbal prefix which appears to be the most important characteristic, whereas in indirect relative sentences the occurrence of a pre-verbal item comparable in shape to Demonstrative (2) is characteristic.

Four post-radical suffixes occur in Kuria, of which -nga is one. A rather larger number of tense signs occurs than for Gusii, but there is no evidence of tense-sign combination. Two negative signs occur as for Gusii, but ti- occurs concomitantly with a post-verbal item 'hai'. Tenses characterized by an initial ni/e- are of both Type 1 and 2. Only one pair of tenses has been recorded which differs only by tone-pattern. Time differentiation is asymmetrical, three divisions of future time and two of past time being recognized. Both the direct and indirect relative appear to be associated with a pre-verbal item, differing in tone only from Demonstrative (1).

These observations may now be summarized diagrammatically. It must be stressed that adequate comparative work needs to be supported by phonological studies of the languages concerned. This is not attempted here; instead the tense signs and post-radical suffixes of Kuria and Gusii are taken and numbered — or lettered[9] — and similar designations accorded signs from the other languages according to the data available, providing no more than a pointer for further work:—

From the foregoing it can be argued that, from an overall point of view, Gusii represents the most westerly of the Gĩkũyũ type languages, and the most easterly of what might be termed VCV-prefix languages.[10] Further, since in general terms Gusii must probably be regarded as closer to Gĩkũyũ, one should regard the VCV element as resulting from contact with the eastward looking languages of E10 and E30. One might, indeed, split up Zone E into a Western and an Eastern section, with Gusii in an intermediate position. Such a suggestion leads immediately to further questions, which are beyond the scope of the present study but which can at least be posed. For example, do the languages of this Eastern section form an isolated group, or can one discern affinities with languages to the south, in neighbouring Zones F and G? Similarly, can one show any general affinities between languages of the Western section and those of neighbouring Zones such as D and M? Here, one is interested not merely in the linguistic relationship, but in the broader relationships between languages as spoken by groups of people, which might give indications as to earlier dispositions and migrations. Non linguistic evidence may here prove of value: where linguistic facts suggest a link, history or social anthropology may corroborate it. Initially, however, the linguist is responsible for collecting and presenting the linguistic facts in as detailed and accurate a manner as possible: only when this has been achieved will there be any chance of his being able to ask really productive questions.
